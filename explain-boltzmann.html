Choosing octaves for chord tones by pretending the notes are a gas

hsjoihs
2026-07-04

The problem

A chord like Cmaj7 only signifies that the pitch classes C, E, G and B. It lacks information on which octave each note should sound in. To actually play the progression on the piano roll, I have to voice it, i.e. decide that this C is C4, that E is E4, and so on. There are a lot of ways to do this, and I wanted a principled (and slightly whimsical) way of picking one.

So I borrowed the machinery of statistical mechanics. I give every possible voicing an energy, where lower energy means “more pleasant / more likely”, and then I draw a voicing at random, preferring the low-energy ones. Nothing here is deep physics; it is just a convenient recipe for turning some musical preferences into probabilities.

TL;DR

  1. Pretend that each candidate voicing is a physical state with an energy \(E\).
  2. At a chosen temperature \(T\), pick a voicing with probability proportional to \(e^{-E/T}\) (the Boltzmann distribution).
  3. The energy rewards notes for sitting in a comfortable central register, for moving smoothly from the previous chord, and it punishes harsh clashes.
  4. To avoid trying every combination, place the notes one at a time.

What “energy” and “temperature” mean here

In physics, a system kept at temperature \(T\) is found in a state of energy \(E\) with probability

\[ P \;\propto\; e^{-E/T} \]

The intuition is that:

  • Low-energy states are more likely.
  • When \(T\) is small, that \(e^{-E/T}\) heavily favours the very lowest energy (the system freezes into its best state).
  • When \(T\) is large, \(E/T\) is tiny for everything, so all states become almost equally likely (the system melts into chaos).

The energy of a voicing

Write a voicing as a list of pitches \(p_1, p_2, \dots\) measured in semitones (I use MIDI numbers, so C4 = 60). The energy is a sum of three kinds of term.

1. A comfortable register (the “potential”)

I want the upper voices to cluster around the middle of the range rather than drift off to the extremes. So I put each note in a parabolic well (valley) centered on F♯4 (\(c = 66\), the middle of the range I draw):

\[ V_{\text{pot}}(p) \;=\; \frac{(p-c)^2}{2S^2} \]

A note pays more energy the further it strays from F♯4, and quadratically so. The number \(S\) is the width of the well: a bigger \(S\) is a wider, gentler valley that tolerates notes further from the center.

2. Avoiding harsh clashes (the penalties)

Some pairs of simultaneous notes sound rough. For two notes \(d\) semitones apart I add:

\(d = 1\)a bare minor 2nda small penalty \(\varepsilon_2\)
\(d = 13, 25, \dots\)a minor 9th or wider (a minor 2nd stacked above one or more octaves)a large penalty \(\varepsilon_9\)
everything else—no penalty

Musicians call a minor 9th above a chord tone an avoid note. Meanwhile the major 7th (\(d=11\)), which is the same two pitch classes spaced the other way, is left completely free, because it is considered unproblematic in the music community.

Two remarks:

  • A minor 9th (\(d=13\)) is punished harder than a minor 2nd, (\(d=1\)). This decision is based on the following claim by Toydora:

    「半音でぶつかっている音の響きの良さっていうのは、長七度が一番良くて、短二度はやや悪くて、で短九度は最悪っていう順番になっています」

    —Toydora, 【音楽理論】謎のテンション "10th" とは？ 実はルートはアヴォイド・ノート⁉ テンション＆アヴォイドの常識を覆す at 30:31

  • Both penalties are switched off entirely if the chord already contains a tritone (two notes six semitones apart). Many textbooks allow the minor 9th when placed on dominant-type chords that already live on tension by means of a tritone interval.

3. Smooth voice leading

From the second chord onward, I reward voices for not jumping around. For a note \(p\), let \(q^{*}\) be the nearest note of the previous chord. I add

\[ V_{\text{lead}}(p) \;=\; \lambda \,\bigl| p - q^{*} \bigr| \]

so a held note (distance 0) is free, a step costs a little, and a leap costs a lot. This is what makes a voice glide by a semitone or a whole tone instead of teleporting across the staff.

Putting it together

For one chord, coupled to the previous one, the total energy is

\[ E \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_i V_{\text{pot}}(p_i)}_{\text{stay central}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{i < j} \text{penalty}\bigl(|p_i-p_j|\bigr)}_{\text{no clashes}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \sum_i \bigl|p_i - q^{*}_i\bigr|}_{\text{move smoothly}} \]

and the voicing is drawn with probability \(\propto e^{-E/T}\). The defaults are \(S = 3.4\) semitones and \(T = 0.4\), with \(\varepsilon_2 = 13 \lambda \) and \(\varepsilon_9 = 21 \lambda \). (13 and 21 are taken from the Fibonacci sequence, since it being approximately equally spaced in the logarithmic space makes it useful for eyeballing.)

Placing the notes one at a time

Naïvely sampling a whole chord “at once” from \(e^{-E/T}\) would mean weighing every combination of octaves for every note, causing a combinatorial explosion. Instead I place the notes sequentially: I fix one note’s octave, then choose the next note’s octave conditioned on the ones already placed, and so on. Each single choice is a small Boltzmann draw over just a handful of octaves, so the cost grows only linearly.

The order matters a little, so I settle the decisive notes first (the ones with an obvious best octave) and leave the ambiguous ones for last, once their neighbours are already down to guide them.

Example. In Cmaj7, E, G and B each have a clear favourite octave near F♯4, so they go down first. The root C is exactly a tritone away from F♯4, so C4 and C5 are equally comfortable in the well. Placing C last lets the already-placed B4 break the tie: C5 would clash with B4 (a minor 2nd), so C4 wins. That is how the familiar close voicing C4–E4–G4–B4 emerges most of the time.

The bass

The bass line is placed independently of the voices above it: I take the chord’s root and drop it into its own low parabolic well (centered around E2) by the same Boltzmann rule. It does not take part in the clash or voice-leading bookkeeping; it just anchors the chord from below.

Why the bell curve?

If you ignore the clash and voice-leading terms, a single note feels only the well \(V_{\text{pot}}\), so its probability across octaves is

\[ P(p) \;\propto\; e^{-V_{\text{pot}}(p)/T} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{(p-c)^2}{2\,S^2 T}\right), \]

which is a Gaussian (a bell curve) centered on F♯4. That is exactly the shape drawn sideways to the left of the piano roll.

Notice the width of that bell is \(S\sqrt{T}\), not \(S\).

  • \(S\) is a fixed property of the well (how the register preference is shaped). In the UI I denote it as Force closed vs. Allow open.
  • \(T\) is the temperature (how much thermal jiggling blurs that preference).
Turning either one up makes the bell wider and the octave choices looser, but they are different knobs. Fixing the bell shape while raising the temperature means making both the stabilization by voice leading and the avoidance of harsh intervals less effective.

Flaws

This is a toy, not a theory of voicing. The sequential placement only approximates the true joint distribution \(e^{-E/T}\), and its result depends on the order I chose. The energy terms and their constants are picked by taste, not derived from anything. And “smoothness measured to the nearest previous note” is a crude stand-in for real voice leading, which cares about which voice goes where.
